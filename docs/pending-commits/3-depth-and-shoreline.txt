Land turned to water because the depth buffer could not tell them apart

Mark: "the land doesn't stay land as you move, the pixels change, nothing seems
fixed in place ... the edges of them get blurred or change to something else
like land to water." Two independent causes, both arithmetic, both now measured
rather than guessed at.

ONE: DEPTH PRECISION.

A 24-bit fixed-point depth buffer spends its precision near the camera, and how
badly is set by the near:far ratio:

    dz = z^2 * (far - near) / (far * near * 2^24)

  world camera (near 3, far 120000)    1 km 0.020 m   10 km  1.987 m   26 km 13.431 m
  city  camera (near 0.1, far 12000)   1 km 0.596 m   10 km 59.604 m

Roads are drawn 0.9 m above the terrain, so from about 7 km out on the world
camera -- and from roughly one kilometre on the city camera, inside the city it
exists to show -- the buffer genuinely cannot separate a road from the ground it
sits on, and which one draws is decided by rounding, per pixel, changing as the
camera moves.

The same number explains the coastline directly: at 13.4 m of resolution, the
sea plane at y=0 and any land within 13 m of sea level are the same depth. The
water drew over the land. "Land to water" was literal.

Both renderers now request logarithmicDepthBuffer, which spreads precision over
the whole range: 7 mm at 10 km on the world camera, 285x better, 741x at the far
edge. Raising `near` would also work on paper (near=30 gives 0.199 m at 10 km)
but clips anything within 30 m of the camera, which breaks the ground-level
flythrough. The cost of the log buffer is the early-Z optimisation.

Verified by rendering, not by reasoning: scripts/shoot.mjs through SwiftShader,
before and after, same view. Before, the whole landmass sits under a blue-grey
film with water bleeding across it; after, the land is opaque and the coast is a
line. Zero console errors, so bloom, the vignette and GradeShader all survive --
which was the open question, and the answer is that the only hand-written shader
in public/ is a full-screen ShaderPass that never touches scene depth.

TWO: THE BEACH WAS NEVER SAMPLED.

The entire beach lives in a 3.8 m window of height -- tide strip to -0.6, dry
sand to +1.6, dune grass to +3.2. The outer terrain mesh samples every 162.5 m.
On any ordinary coastal slope no vertex ever lands inside that window:
consecutive samples come out at something like -20 m and +25 m, so the band
table is asked for shelf blue and coastal green and never once for sand. The GPU
then interpolates between those two across the whole triangle.

So the blur was not a soft edge. It was a beach that did not exist, replaced by
a 162 m gradient from sea colour to grass colour -- and it moved with the camera
because which vertices got sampled changed.

The band table is now evaluated against the INTERPOLATED height in the fragment
shader, so the beach lands exactly where the surface crosses sea level, at
screen resolution, whatever the vertex spacing. The GLSL is COMPILED FROM THE
SAME EXPORTED GROUND_BANDS the CPU path uses -- a colour table maintained in two
languages is a drift defect with a delay fuse, and this file has paid for that
before. Sequential mixes are exactly equivalent to bandColor's find-then-blend:
below a band's window its t is 0 and the mix is a no-op, above it t is 1 and the
colour is replaced outright.

Stock MeshStandardMaterial patched via onBeforeCompile rather than replaced by a
ShaderMaterial, so it keeps three.js lighting, shadows, tone mapping and the
logarithmic depth chunks the change above depends on.

Two new attributes carry what the shader cannot know: the noise multipliers, so
a per-pixel beach has the same grain as the ground around it instead of reading
as a flat painted stripe; and a weight that refuses sand on cliffed shore and
through built ground, which are the same judgements the vertex colour already
makes, expressed as a weight rather than a blend.

Rendered result: every island now carries a continuous sand rim of consistent
width. In the previous shot they had no beach at all.

THE GUARD.

Removing logarithmicDepthBuffer does not throw, does not fail to compile, and
changes nothing in a screenshot taken near the camera -- it only makes distant
geometry flicker again, which reads as "the graphics are a bit off" and took a
long time to trace the first time. test/rendererStatic.test.ts now counts
WebGLRenderer constructions against the flag, so a second renderer cannot appear
without one. Mutation-verified: dropped from each file in turn, the test goes
red both times.

THREE: THE SEA DID NOT KNOW HOW DEEP IT WAS.

Mark, on the same problem from the other side: "should the land be a solid mass
that goes to the core like the earth, and the water sit on top of that ... this
will allow for the water to lap up on the beach like real waves."

The base was already there and is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to
assume otherwise: the sea bed is not a separate object, it is the terrain
surface continuing below zero, walled to bedrock at -175 and floored by the
abyss plane. The ocean genuinely sits in a basin. What was missing is that the
WATER had no idea any of it was there -- one flat PlaneGeometry four worlds
wide, constant 0.62 opacity, a tiled normal map, and no knowledge of where the
shore was. It could not shallow toward a beach, clear over sand, or put foam on
a waterline. The precomputed surf ribbon is the workaround for that, and it is
geometry offset from a polygon, resolution-bound exactly like the old beach.

Filling the land with triangles is not what fixes this and would cost millions
of them nobody can see. What fixes it is the same move that fixed the beach: the
sea now samples the height field as a texture and reads its own depth per pixel,
which drives shallow-water colour, transparency that clears toward the sand, and
a foam band that sits on the true waterline and breathes across it.

512 x 512, chosen by measurement: 256 gives 152 m texels, 512 gives 76 m, 1024
gives 38 m. 512 is the first resolution FINER than the 162 m land mesh beneath
it, so the water is never the coarser of the two.

The cost, and what was done about it. In plain Node the bake is 477 ms. In the
real renderer it took generateWorld from 5,568 ms to 8,156 ms -- 2.6 s, five
times the Node figure, which is exactly why the Node figure was not treated as
the answer. Earlier work cut this build from 6.02 s to 2.36 s and handing a
fifth of that back for something invisible in the first frame is a bad trade, so
the bake is deferred. But deferring a 2.6 s block only moves it to where it
reads as the page hanging for no reason, so it is also SLICED: 32 rows per idle
callback. Measured back at 6,327 ms, and the sea starts flat and gains its depth
response a beat later. A depth map that never arrives is a flat sea, not a
broken one.

Verified by rendering both ways. The deferred path produces a frame identical to
before, which is the point -- first paint is untouched. Forcing the bake inline
produces turquoise shallows around every shore, water that clears over the sand,
and a swash line following the coast.

WHAT IS NOT FIXED. The foreground sea still shows the tiling seams of its normal
map, which is a separate defect and predates all of this. And the swash animates
the foam band's width rather than moving actual water up the sand -- it reads as
a tide line breathing, not as a breaking wave. Both are honest gaps, written
down rather than implied away.

470/470 node tests, 12/12 worker tests, tsc --noEmit clean.

One note on the guard: rendererStatic.test.ts flagged requestIdleCallback as an
undefined name, and it was right to -- it did not know the global. Adding it to
the list is not a loosening; the check is for names that exist nowhere, and that
one exists. It is used behind a typeof guard because Safari lacked it for years.
