Place, move, remove -- and a move that is refused puts the thing back

The land could be asked and the world could remember, and nothing connected
them. This is that connection, and it is the only one: ask, and on a yes,
record. Nothing else should reserve ground directly, for the same reason nothing
should draw a bench by typing its size into a BoxGeometry call.

WHY ASKING AND RECORDING HAVE TO BE ONE OPERATION

Every defect this project has found is the same shape: two subsystems with
private beliefs about the same ground, and nothing to reconcile them. A stadium
at -7.0 m in the water. 389 plots inside feature footprints. 27 lamp posts
inside benches. Two placement loops walking the same footway, neither able to
see the other.

The cure is not more checking. It is that checking and committing cannot be
separated. If a caller can reserve without asking, or ask without reserving, the
two drift apart again and nothing detects it.

THREE VERBS

  place(model, x, z)        ask, and on a yes, record
  placeNear(model, near)    spiral outward, and report how far it had to move
  remove(id, t)             close the interval
  move(model, id, x, z)     remove, then place -- and restore on refusal

placeNear reports `moved` because a thing that ends up 3 km from where it was
wanted is a fact about the world, not a successful placement. The airport moved
450 m, and that number is the difference between a considered decision and a
coincidence.

Refusals are counted by reason rather than swallowed. A run that placed 4,000
things and refused 12,000 is worth being able to see, and the SHAPE of the
refusals says which rule is doing the work -- which is how "in a waterway" and
"cliff" counts first showed the plot generator fighting the terrain.

TIME, ACTUALLY USED

Every reservation has carried `since` and `until` since the registry was
written, and the only way to take something away was release(), which deletes
the record. So the world could say what is here, could in principle say what was
here a year ago, and in practice could never say the second one about anything
removed. An unused dimension is cheap; a dimension that is carried and then
quietly discarded is a false claim.

close(id, t) ends a thing's life at t. release(id) still exists and still
erases, for correcting a reservation that should never have been made. Those are
different acts and collapsing them would cost the fourth dimension entirely.
Closing at or before something's `since` throws, because an interval true at no
time at all looks correct in the data and cannot be reasoned about afterwards.

A REFUSED MOVE PUTS THE THING BACK

This is the property worth having in an editing tool. Without it, a failed move
DELETES what it was moving -- and it happens precisely when the user is already
being told no.

The first implementation got it wrong in an instructive way. It closed the
original, tried the new position, and on refusal re-reserved a copy of the old
entry. That leaves the closed original in place AND adds a second record beside
it, so the world believes the same hut stood in the same spot twice with
different lifetimes, and every later query is answered by whichever comes first.
Caught by a test asserting "exactly one record, not zero and not two" -- it left
two. The registry now has reopen(), the symmetric partner of close(), and a
refused move leaves no trace at all, including in the history.

VERIFIED BY MUTATION

  place() reserves even when the land said no  -> 5 tests fail
  remove() erases instead of closing           -> 2 fail
  a refused move does not restore              -> 1 fail
  close() accepts a time before since          -> 1 fail

WHAT THIS DELIBERATELY DOES NOT DO

It does not decide where anything goes. place is told a position; placeNear
searches from a preference. Neither invents a layout and neither knows what a
city is. That is a layout engine, written in terms of these verbs and the model
library, and it is the next thing.
